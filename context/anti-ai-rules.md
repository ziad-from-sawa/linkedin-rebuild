# Anti-AI Writing Rules

These are **HARD RULES**. They apply to every sentence, in every section, for every creator. They override voice-profile tendencies when in conflict. A draft that violates any of these is a failure, even if the voice match is otherwise strong. Audit every sentence against this list before returning output.

---

## 1. Em-dashes are forbidden
Never use the em-dash (—) or en-dash (–). Replace with a period, comma, colon, or restructure. **Zero tolerance.** A single em-dash is a failure.

- ❌ "I'm Cat — and welcome to the newsletter."
- ✅ "I'm Cat. Welcome to the newsletter."
- ❌ "Three things to know — all grounded in the data."
- ✅ "Three things to know. All of them grounded in the data."

## 2. "It's not X, it's Y" construction is forbidden (and all its siblings)
The single most recognizable AI tell. Forbidden variants:
- "It's not X, it's Y"
- "It isn't about X, it's about Y" / "It's not about X, it's about Y"
- "That's not X. That's Y."
- "X is not just A, it's B"
- "You're not buying X, you're buying Y"
- "This isn't (just) a/an X. It's a Y."

**Fix:** state what the thing IS, directly. Skip the contrast. If the contrast matters, use "while" or "instead" in normal prose.

- ❌ "That's not education. That's marketing."
- ✅ "That's marketing dressed up as education."
- ❌ "It's not about the app. It's about the habit."
- ✅ "The habit is what matters here, not the app."

## 3. Revelation Hook is forbidden
Do not position yourself as exposing a hidden truth.
- "Here's the truth nobody tells you about X..."
- "What nobody admits about X..."
- "Here's what they won't tell you..."
- "No one's saying this out loud, but..."
- "The thing nobody talks about..."

**Fix:** just say the thing. Lead with the observation.

- ❌ "Here's the truth nobody tells you about AI: the loudest voices are selling courses."
- ✅ "The loudest voices in AI right now are selling courses."

## 4. Elliptical Setup (rhetorical-question fragment) is forbidden
No intrigue via a fragmentary question followed by the answer. **Zero tolerance.**
- "The best part? You don't lift a finger."
- "The wild thing? It worked."
- "The only catch? Time."

**Fix:** state the point as a regular sentence.

- ❌ "The best part? It took ten minutes."
- ✅ "It took ten minutes, which was the whole point."

## 5. Great Reframe & Philosophical Reduction are forbidden
Do not balance two clauses with a moral/conceptual flip, or reduce a big idea to a poetic contradiction.
- ❌ "Everyone's chasing attention. Few are earning trust."
- ❌ "Speed doesn't win. Momentum does."
- ❌ "AI isn't replacing people. It's replacing waiting."

**Fix:** express one coherent thought. Skip the paradox.
- ✅ "Trust is what I am after, and it takes longer to earn than attention."

## 6. No corporate or marketing vocabulary

**Hard ban (the marketing/hype core).** Never use: leverage, utilize, streamline, optimize, seamless, robust, scalable, holistic, frictionless, ecosystem, tapestry, deep dive, unpack, delve, stakeholder, synergy, cutting-edge, next-generation, game-changer, paradigm shift, low-hanging fruit, move the needle, circle back, actionable insights, bleeding edge, pain point, touchpoint, thought leadership, essentially. These have no plain-prose home; they are marketing residue. Say what the thing does in plain terms instead of hyping it: "I added a button" not "I supercharged the workflow."

**Context-dependent (cut only when inflating, keep the plain literal sense).** empower, bandwidth (as capacity), landscape, journey, alignment, quietly. Each has an honest literal use: "empower citizens" as in give them power, "the political landscape," "a long journey home," "she said it quietly." Flag and check each: cut it where it is hyping, keep it where it names the plain thing.

**AI hype verbs (banned in the hype sense).** unlock, unleash, elevate, supercharge, revolutionize, transform, redefine, reimagine, harness, foster, cultivate, spearhead, usher in, embark, navigate (as in "navigate the complexities"), tap into, dive into. These are banned when used to hype. They are allowed in a plain, literal sense ("redefine success" as in propose a different definition, "cultivate a habit"). The test is whether the verb is inflating the claim or just naming the action.

**Soft flag (connective adverbs, overuse only).** however, thus, therefore, indeed, moreover, furthermore, arguably, ultimately, additionally, consequently, notably. These are ordinary educated English, not corporate-speak, and plain prose uses them as quiet pivots. Do not auto-cut them. Prefer the lighter word where it reads the same ("so" over "therefore," "but" over "however"), and watch for *overuse*: more than a couple of these per edition, or a paragraph leaning on them as scaffolding, is the tell. One well-placed "however" mid-sentence is fine.

- ✅ "Her attraction to me, however, was less obvious." (quiet mid-sentence pivot, fine)
- ❌ A run of "Moreover ... Furthermore ... Thus ... Ultimately ..." carrying paragraph transitions (overuse, rewrite)

## 7. No formal academic transitions or summaries
Never use the summary scaffolding: "In conclusion," "To summarize," "In summary," "It's worth noting that," "It's important to note," "As previously mentioned," "To put it simply." This is dead essay-template residue. Just end, or just say the thing. **Allowed:** the spoken hinge "In short" from the sentence-craft menu. It lands a mid-piece summary in conversational register and is not the essay-template residue this rule targets. For a closing beat, prefer an informal label or no label at all ("One last thought," or simply stop).

**Allowed as a genuine two-sided contrast:** "On the other hand" and "In contrast" are fine when they introduce a real second side of a comparison, especially paired ("On one hand ... on the other hand ..."). They are not AI filler in that use. Avoid them only when they are decorative, standing in for a turn that is not actually a contrast.

- ✅ "On one hand, we naturally seek prosperity. On the other hand, success is often marred by a materialism we find repellent."
- ❌ "On the other hand," dropped in front of a sentence that does not contrast with anything before it.

## 8. No AI-pattern preambles in the output
Never begin with meta-commentary like "Here's the draft:", "I've written a draft that...", "Below is the newsletter:". The first line of output is the chosen subject line, the second line is blank, the third line is the actual email copy. Nothing before it.

## 9. No negation-cascade fragments ("Not X. Not Y. Not Z. Just Q.")
Do not stack multiple "Not ___." fragments to build rhythm, then resolve with "Just ___." Forbidden regardless of how good the rhythm sounds.
- ❌ "This isn't a side hustle. Not a course. Not a framework. Just a newsletter."
- ❌ "No hype. No jargon. No funnels. Just writing."

The tell: two or more consecutive sentences beginning with "Not"/"No" + noun phrase, optionally resolved by "Just."

**Fix:** rewrite as a single sentence with inline enumeration, or describe the thing directly.

**Exception:** a single "Not ___." fragment in isolation is fine. The rule targets the *cascade* (2+ consecutive).

## 10. Don't split one continuous thought into "A. And B." (the comma-splice tic)
The tell is one thought broken by a period where a comma/colon/semicolon belongs, then rejoined with a sentence-initial "And"/"But"/"Or"/"So". Ban only that. A sentence-initial "And"/"But"/"So" that opens a real new beat, turn, or re-entry is good writing, so use it freely.

- ❌ "I read every single one. And I'll write back." (comma belongs)
- ✅ "She promised to call. But she didn't." (real turn)
- ✅ "So here's where it gets interesting." (opens a new beat)
- ✅ "And that's the part everyone misses." (deliberate landing)

**Quick test:** replace the leading conjunction's period with a comma. If it collapses into one smooth clause with no rhythmic loss, it was a continuation, so use the comma. If the comma would bury a real beat or turn, keep the period and the conjunction.

## 11. No "No X, no Y" negation stacking (2+ items)
Separate from rule 9. Rule 9 owns the period-separated fragment cascade ("No hype. No jargon."); this owns the inline comma form, two or more "no ___" items stacked inside one breath.
- ❌ "no hype, no courses in the link-in-bio"
- ❌ "no jargon, no funnels, no BS"

**Fix:** pick the sharpest one and cut the rest, or rewrite as a single positive statement.
- ✅ "I'm not going to sell you a course."

## 12. No dramatic-colon usage
The AI "colon tic": short clause + colon + short clause, used to punch up a point.
- ❌ "The math is simple: one filter cleans every cup."
- ❌ "The takeaway is obvious: most filters miss microplastics."

**Fix:** use a period or merge into one flowing sentence.
- ✅ "The math is simple. One filter cleans every cup."

**Allowed colons:** introducing a list, numbered steps ("**Step 1: Pick a task**"), dialogue attribution, technical formats (URLs, times, headers, YAML), CTA/subject-prefix labels.

**Quick test:** if the colon sits between two full sentences and a period would lose no meaning, it's the AI tic, so use the period.

## 13. Filler intensifiers: flag and test, do not auto-cut
"actually," "really," "genuinely," "literally," "truly," "basically" are usually reflexive padding, but not always. Do not cut them on sight. Run the read-aloud test on each, and keep the ones that are doing real work.

**Quick test (primary):** read the sentence aloud without the word. If it lands unchanged, the word was AI residue, so cut it. If dropping it changes or weakens the meaning, keep it.
- "what's actually happening" → "what's happening" (cut: no change)
- "I really think" → "I think" (cut: no change)
- "it's basically a newsletter" → "it's a newsletter" (cut: no change)

**Keep when the word carries meaning, not just emphasis:**
- "I mean that literally" marks the literal/figurative boundary, so it is load-bearing.
- "actually most likely to keep working" flags a counterintuitive finding, so it earns its place.
- "Basically, I want them to have a map" is a sentence-initial discourse marker setting a spoken register; keep it where it does that, cut it where it is pure padding ("it's basically a newsletter").

A meaningful intensifier (literal/figurative marker or counterintuitive flag) survives the read-aloud test by definition. Cap the reflexive ones at one per paragraph.

## 14. "real" as a wellness/authenticity signal is forbidden
"Real" as a value adjective is an AI tell: "real food," "real writers," "real people," "the real reason," "real talk," "real one." Cut it or replace with something specific.
- "real food" → name the category (whole foods, beef and eggs)
- "real writers" → "human writers"
- "the real reason" → the actual specific reason

**Factual use is fine** ("a real URL from the search results," "real numbers"). The ban is on "real" as a tonal/authenticity signal.

## 15. "stuff," "things," and "whatever" hand-waves: extremely limited usage
"Stuff" and "things" are vague filler nouns that dodge the specific word. So is the "whatever" hand-wave ("whatever we pour on top," "whatever comes out of the faucet"). Default to naming the actual thing. Cut them wherever a concrete noun fits.
- "all the stuff you eat" → "everything you eat" / name the foods
- "three things to know" → "three habits to know" / name the category
- "this stuff works" → "this protocol works"
- "whatever we pour on top" → "the creamer" / "milk and sugar"

**Allowed:** at most one per email, and only where vagueness is the point. If a specific noun reads naturally in its place, it was filler, so replace it.

## 16. "Whole" as filler is forbidden
"Whole" used as filler emphasis, in any position. This covers the wrap-up summarizer ("That's the whole point," "That's kind of my whole thing," "the whole reason I started this," "the whole game/deal/story") and the inline intensifier ("the whole drink," "its whole design," "the whole foundation"). Run the delete test: if the sentence means the same without "whole," the word was filler, so cut it or say the specific amount.
- ❌ "That's the whole point."
- ❌ "That's the whole reason I started this."
- ✅ "I started this because I was tired of guessing."
- ❌ "water is nearly the whole drink by volume" → ✅ "coffee is nearly all water"
- ❌ "whose whole design is to push hot water through plastic" → ✅ "designed to push hot water through plastic"

**Allowed:** the literal food senses only ("whole milk," "whole grains," "whole foods"), and genuine totality where "whole" changes the meaning ("she read the whole study").

## 17. Three-beat cadence (triads and A, B, and C lists): capped hard at one per edition
Three-beat cadence is a strong AI tell when it becomes a reflex. This includes **both** forms:

1. **The sentence triad**: three consecutive sentences built on the same shape or length to manufacture rhythm.
2. **The internal triple**: a single sentence leaning on a polished three-item build, especially `A, B, and C`, `A, B, or C`, or repeated gerund triples like `growing, learning, building`.

These are **not** fully banned, because an occasional rule-of-three can land. But they are **capped hard**: across both forms combined, allow **at most one** deliberate three-beat cadence per edition. If a second one shows up anywhere in the draft, rewrite it.
- ❌ "It's cheap. It's fast. It's everywhere." (three parallel short sentences as filler rhythm)
- ❌ "You skip breakfast. You crash by noon. You reach for sugar." (parallel build, used as the paragraph's default cadence)
- ❌ "You're growing, learning new tools, and building fast." (the polished internal triple cadence)
- ❌ "self-promotion, office politics, or playing a game" used again two paragraphs later in a second `A, B, or C` build

The tell: repeated three-beat rhythm, whether spread across sentences or packed inside one long sentence. Two items is fine. A clean single list can be fine. The problem is the recurring three-part beat that starts sounding written.

**Fix:** break the three-beat cadence. Merge two items, cut one item, vary the sentence shape, or turn the list into a more natural clause so the rhythm is not a metronome. Do **not** solve it by chopping the thought into staccato fragments. Keep one grounded sentence if the idea wants one; just remove the polished three-beat pattern.
- ✅ "It's cheap and everywhere, and it works fast, which is exactly why it's so hard to quit."

**Allowed:** a single, intentional three-beat move used once for genuine emphasis, where the rhythm is the point and not a habit. If a second sentence triad or internal triple shows up anywhere in the same edition, rewrite it.

## 18. No copula avoidance (say "is," don't dress it up)
Reaching for a fancier linking verb instead of a plain "is/are" to sound weightier: "serves as," "stands as," "acts as," "functions as," "represents," "constitutes," "boasts," "features," "comes packed with." "boasts"/"features" in particular read like product copy.
- ❌ "Breakfast serves as the anchor of your day."
- ❌ "The recipe boasts twelve grams of protein."

**Fix:** swap back to "is/are," or better, use the real verb of what the thing does.
- ✅ "Breakfast anchors your day."
- ✅ "The recipe has twelve grams of protein."

**Reference (the pattern in the wild):**
- ❌ "Gallery 825 serves as LAAA's exhibition space for contemporary art. The gallery features four separate spaces and boasts over 3,000 square feet."
- ✅ "Gallery 825 is LAAA's exhibition space for contemporary art. The gallery has four rooms totaling 3,000 square feet."

**Flag, don't blind-replace:** "features"/"has" sometimes do honest work ("the episode features a guest"). Check each; cut only where the verb is inflating a plain "is."

## 19. No vague attribution ("experts say," "studies show")
Sourcing a claim to nameless authority instead of a real source: "experts say," "studies show," "research suggests," "science says," "doctors agree," "many believe," "it's widely known." In a fact-grounded newsletter this is doubly bad: it fakes authority *and* is the exact phrasing that precedes a fabricated citation, which is the cardinal failure.
- ❌ "Studies show seed oils drive inflammation."
- ❌ "Experts agree breakfast matters."

**Fix:** name the actual source from the client's fact source, or frame it plainly as the creator's own observed take. If you can name neither, the claim isn't grounded, so cut it.
- ✅ "In the podcast, Kyle walked through the study that tied seed oils to inflammation."

**Reference (the pattern in the wild):**
- ❌ "Experts believe it plays a crucial role in the regional ecosystem."
- ✅ "The Haolai River supports several endemic fish species, according to a 2019 survey by the Chinese Academy of Sciences." (specific source, date, institution)

## 20. No significance inflation
Announcing that something is a landmark instead of showing what it did: "marking a pivotal moment," "marks a turning point," "a defining shift," "cannot be overstated," "history in the making," "a watershed," "the dawn of." (The corporate cousins "game-changer" and "paradigm shift" are already hard-banned under rule 6; this catches the same impulse in its non-corporate disguises.) Watch words: "is a testament/reminder, vital/significant/crucial/pivotal/key role/moment, underscores/highlights its importance." (The "stands/serves as" copula tell lives in rule 18, not here.)

The fix is not a smaller superlative. **Delete the significance claim and put the underlying fact in its place.** State what the thing did and let the reader judge whether it mattered.

**Reference (the pattern in the wild):**
- ❌ "The Statistical Institute of Catalonia was officially established in 1989, marking a pivotal moment in the evolution of regional statistics in Spain."
- ✅ "The Statistical Institute of Catalonia was established in 1989 to collect and publish regional statistics independently from Spain's national statistics office."

**In newsletter voice:**
- ❌ "This swap marks a turning point in how you eat."
- ✅ "This swap is the one I still do every morning, two years later." (a concrete fact that shows it mattered, without announcing it)

## 21. No stock phrases
Banned wherever they appear, as sentences, lead-ins, or asides: "Here's the thing." "Let that sink in." "But here's the kicker." "Make no mistake." "Here's what most people miss." "I can't stop thinking about." "Game changer" and "move the needle" are already banned under rule 6. These are templates standing in for a thought. ("Sweet spot" naming a real optimum or overlap, as in "the sweet spot of overlap between interest and reward," is ordinary English, not a stock phrase.)

**Fix:** delete the phrase and say the point directly.
- ❌ "Here's the thing. Most filters don't remove microplastics."
- ✅ "Most filters don't remove microplastics."

## 22. Mix sentence lengths: no staccato runs
Runs of short sentences in a staccato meant to sound dramatic are a tell. Real writing mixes long and short. One short sentence landing a real beat is fine; three or more consecutive short sentences or fragments used as rhythm is the pattern to break.

**Fix:** merge into one flowing sentence, keeping the one beat that earns its period.
- ❌ "Tomorrow morning. Same as ever. Just cleaner."
- ✅ "Tomorrow morning will look the same as ever, just cleaner."

## 23. No dramatic emotional proclamations
"This is wild." "I was floored." "I was a wreck." "That was the moment I knew." These *tell* emotion instead of showing it.

**Fix:** show the fact or moment that carried the emotion and let the reader feel it. If the emotion needs stating, state it plainly inside the sentence ("that surprised me"), never as a standalone proclamation.
- ❌ "I was floored."
- ✅ "I read the number twice, then a third time, sure I had misplaced a decimal."

## 24. No decorative metaphors (fake vividness), no oblique stand-ins
Metaphors meant to sound human that sound like an impersonation: "planted a seed," "her story rhymed with mine," "the 3am wakeup call," "passengers riding along in the cup." A metaphor is allowed only when it explains a concept more simply than the literal statement would (the sprained-ankle analogy for inflammation earns its place; "a nation running on the bean" does not).

Same ban for the **oblique stand-in**: a clever image used *instead of naming the thing*, forcing the reader to decode it. If the sentence only makes sense after translating it back into the plain version, write the plain version.
- ❌ "My concerns are with what happens between the bag and the first sip."
- ✅ "My concerns are with the water, the machine, the cup, and the creamer."
- ❌ "everything the coffee meets on its way to us"

**Fix:** say the judgment or fact literally; name the actual things.

## 25. Say it once: no announcing the point, no throat-clearing, no re-explaining
Three forms of the same disease:
- **Announcing instead of making the point:** "That's the takeaway." "That's the shift." "That matters." "What stuck with me is..." "I like that spirit." Just make the point.
- **Throat-clearing (announcing your own speech act before the point):** "Let me say up front that I come in peace." "Let me be clear." "I'll be honest." "Don't get me wrong." "Full disclosure." The sentence announces that a statement is coming instead of being the statement. Delete it; the next sentence already does the work.
- **The re-explain gloss:** stating a thing and immediately restating it in different words that add nothing ("for a simple reason of arithmetic: one filter upgrades every cup"). The second telling re-explains what the reader just read. A restatement that genuinely unpacks a compressed point (unpacking a formula or definition into prose) is fine; an empty echo is the tell.

**Fix:** keep the single strongest version of the sentence and delete the announcement or the restatement.
- ❌ "One good filter upgrades every cup, for a simple reason of arithmetic: it touches all three cups a day."
- ✅ "One good filter upgrades all three cups a day, for years."

## 26. No vague setup resolved by a "because" clause
Making a vague or abstract statement whose actual content arrives only in the trailing "because" explanation. The wind-up is filler; the because-clause is the real sentence.
- ❌ "I don't say this to scare you. I say it to empower you, because every one of these comes with an easy swap."
- ✅ "Every one of these comes with an easy swap."
- ❌ "This is where I tell everyone to begin, with coffee and with everything else, because a cup of coffee is nearly all water."
- ✅ "A cup of coffee is nearly all water, so one good filter upgrades every cup we brew."

**Fix:** delete the vague setup and state the content directly.

## 27. No additive negative parallelism ("not only X, but also Y")
Separate from rule 2. Rule 2 bans the *substitutive* contrast, where Y replaces X ("It's not X, it's Y"). This bans the *additive* version, where the sentence asserts both halves and stacks the second on the first to sound bigger than the plain claim. Forbidden forms:
- "not only X, but also Y" / "not only X, but Y"
- "doesn't just X, it Y's" (and "don't just," "didn't just," "won't just")
- "not only did it work, but it changed everything"

**Fix:** make the one claim you actually mean. If both halves are true, keep the stronger one and cut the other. The stacking is the tell, not either half on its own.
- ❌ "This doesn't just save time, it changes how you work."
- ✅ "This changes how you work." (or, if time is the real point, "This saves about an hour a day.")
- ❌ "Not only is it cheaper, but it's faster too."
- ✅ "It's cheaper and faster."

## 28. No trailing participle padding (the "-ing" tail)
A sentence finishes its real thought, then tacks on a comma plus an "-ing" clause that adds fake analytical weight. The tail restates the point, inflates it, or gestures at a significance the sentence never earned. Watch verbs: creating, cementing, enhancing, highlighting, underscoring, reflecting, symbolizing, contributing to, ensuring, fostering, showcasing, marking, solidifying. (Several of these verbs also show up in rule 20. There the tell is the significance claim itself; here it is the trailing participle structure. Same words, different move.)
- ❌ "The habit stuck, creating a rhythm that carried through my day."
- ❌ "She froze the money, further cementing her commitment to the goal."
- ❌ "The study confirmed it, highlighting the power of self-imposed deadlines."

**Fix:** delete the tail, or promote it to its own sentence with a real verb if it carries a real point.
- ✅ "The habit stuck."
- ✅ "She froze the money. A year later she still could not touch it."

**Allowed:** an "-ing" clause that names a concrete action or physical consequence, not a significance gloss ("She slammed the laptop shut, spilling the coffee"). The test: if the tail says what literally happened next, keep it; if it editorializes about meaning or importance, cut it.

## 29. No elegant variation (say the plain noun again)
Cycling through synonyms to avoid repeating a word is an AI tell. Human writing repeats the plain noun. Generated writing reaches for "the brew," then "the beverage," then "the cup," rather than saying "coffee" four times. The variation itself is the signal.
- ❌ "I quit the coffee. Giving up the brew was hard, but the beverage had ruled my mornings for years."
- ✅ "I quit coffee. Giving up coffee was hard, but coffee had ruled my mornings for years."

**Fix:** pick the one plain word and repeat it. Repetition of the right noun reads as human. Synonym-cycling reads as written.

**Allowed:** a pronoun ("it," "they") is the normal way to avoid repetition and is fine. The ban is on swapping in a second and third *noun* for the same thing to sound varied.

## 30. No forward-looking speculation close
Ending by gesturing at the future to manufacture importance: "where this goes next," "what this could mean," "the potential is enormous," "as more people discover this," "the future of," "ongoing efforts to." It borrows significance from a future that has not happened and that the piece cannot support.
- ❌ "Where this goes next is anyone's guess, but the potential is enormous."
- ❌ "As more people catch on, this could reshape how we all work."

**Fix:** end on a concrete fact, a return to the opening image, or a plain sign-off. Let the piece stop where the thought stops.
- ✅ "These days I no longer split my salads between lunch and dinner. They are no longer $3 either, and I am still not over it." (closes on a concrete image, no gesture at what it all means)
